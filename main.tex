\section{Introduction}

Anomaly detection methods are often rather ad hoc and lack a solid theoretical foundation, or they make strong distributional assumptions that may not hold in practice. Here we propose a general framework for anomaly detection using extreme value theory applied to surprisal values, which are negative log-density values. This approach allows for a unified treatment of anomaly detection in both univariate and multivariate settings, and can be applied to both unconditional and conditional distributions. The basic idea is to assign an anomaly score to each observation based on its surprisal, and then to use extreme value theory to model the tail of the surprisal distribution. This allows us to estimate the probability of observing a surprisal value as extreme or more extreme than a given observation, which can be used to flag anomalies. The surprisal approach to anomaly detection provides a principled way to control the false positive rate when flagging anomalies, by setting a threshold on the estimated tail probabilities.

We show that under weak conditions on the tail of the surprisal distribution, the maximum surprisal converges to one of the three types of extreme value distributions. This justifies fitting a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) to the largest surprisal values, and using it to estimate tail probabilities. We illustrate the approach with several examples, including both simulated and real data.

Let $\bm{y}_1,\dots,\bm{y}_n$ be iid observations on $\mathbb{R}^d$ from a probability distribution $F$. The \emph{surprisal} of observation $\bm{y}_i$ is defined as \citep{Stone2022}
$$
	s_i=-\log f(\bm{y}_i),
$$
where $f$ is either the density or probability mass function of $F$. While these are better known as ``log scores'' in statistics \citep{Good1952-zw}, in this context we prefer the term ``surprisal'' \citep[coined by][]{Tribus61}, as it captures the idea that observations with low density are more ``surprising'' (and therefore potentially anomalous) than those with high density.

Let $G(s) = P(S\le s)$ be the distribution function of the surprisal values, where $S = -\log f(\bm{Y})$ and $\bm{Y}\sim F$. Then, the probability of observing a value with surprisal at least as large as $s$ is given by $1-G(s)$, which leads to a natural way to assign an anomaly score to each observation $\bm{y}_i$:
$$
	p_i = 1-G(s_i) = 1-G(-\log f(\bm{y}_i)).
$$
Hence, one way to detect anomalies is to flag observations with $p_i < \alpha$, giving a false positive rate of $\alpha$ if all observations are from $F$. This is preferable to directly computing the probabilities from the tail of $F$, as it allows for anomalies to occur anywhere in the distribution of $F$, not just in its tails. For example, anomalies may lie between modes in a multimodal distribution, or in otherwise low-density regions of the distribution $F$. The approach also converts the problem of working with a potentially multivariate distribution $F$ to working with a univariate distribution $G$.

We can write
$$
	G(s) = \mathrm{Pr}\{-\log f(Y)\le s\} = \Pr\{ f(Y)\ge e^{-s}\}.
$$
This makes clear that the surprisal probability is related to the highest density regions of the distribution \citep{HDR96}. In fact, every observation with $p_i< \alpha$ lies outside the $100(1-\alpha)$\% HDR region of $F$.

We will usually not know $F$, and so we need to estimate it from data or from theory. Such estimates are particularly fragile in areas of low density, which is precisely where we are looking for anomalies.

To address this, \citet{lookout} proposed estimating $F$ by a leave-one-out kernel density estimate, and then fitting a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) to the most extreme surprisal values. The underlying idea is that even if $F$ is not particularly well-estimated, the tail of the surprisal distribution should still be well-approximated by a GPD. \citet{lookout2} provides some theoretical justification for this approach in the context of kernel density estimation.

Here we extend that idea to allow $F$ to be any well-behaved distribution, including conditional distributions. Specifically, we fit a GPD to the largest $\beta$ of $\{s_i\}_{i=1}^n$, obtaining estimates of the location, scale and shape parameters, $(\mu,\sigma,\xi)$. We calculate the probability of each observation or one more extreme, $p_i = \beta\left[1-P(s_i \mid \hat\mu, \hat\sigma, \hat\xi)\right]$, where $P$ is the distribution function of the GPD. Observations not contained in the largest $\beta$ of $\{s_i\}_{i=1}^n$ are assigned $p_i = \beta$.

To justify this approach, we seek conditions on the surprisal distribution of $S = -\log f(\bm{Y})$, where $\bm{Y}\sim F$, similar to the Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko (FTG) theorem \citep{coles2001introduction}.

\section{Extreme value theory for the surprisal distribution}

Before establishing the main results, we need to place some conditions on the surprisal distribution. We consider three possibilities describing increasingly heavy tails: (1) where $S$ is sub-Gaussian (which occurs when $f$ is bounded above and below); (2) where $S$ is sub-exponential; and (3) where $S$ has a polynomial moment. We state these as three alternative assumptions.

\begin{assumption}[Sub-Gaussian]\label{ass:bounded}
	The random variable $S =-\log f(Y)\in\mathbb{R}$, and satisfies, for all $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$, and some $\nu>0$,
	$$
		\E \exp\{\lambda (S-\E[S])\}\le \exp\{\lambda^2 \nu^2/2\}.
	$$
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Sub-exponential]\label{ass:subbounded}The random variable $S$ is sub-exponential with parameters $\nu$ and $b$, i.e.,
	$$
		\E \exp\{\lambda(S-\E[S])\} \leq \exp\{\lambda^2\nu^2/ 2\} \quad\text{ for all } \quad |\lambda|< 1/b.
	$$
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Polynomial]\label{ass:polybounded}
	The random variable $|S|$ has polynomial moments of order $\alpha\ge 1$; i.e.,
	$\mathbb{E}[|S|^p] \le C^p$ for some $C>0$ such that $C^p-1>0$.
\end{assumption}

Under each of Assumptions \ref{ass:bounded}--\ref{ass:polybounded}, we seek a result on the limiting distribution of the maximum of the surprisals $M_n = \max\{s_1,\dots,s_n\}$. If we can show that this distribution falls within the class of extreme value distributions, then we can fit a Generalized Pareto Distribution to the largest surprisal values \citep{coles2001introduction}.

\subsection{$S$ has sub-Gaussian tails}

Let's first assume that the surprisals are well-behaved and have a sub-Gaussian tail. \cref{ass:bounded} will be satisfied in cases where the density $f$ is bounded both above and below, such as with mass functions on a finite set. However, it may not be satisfied if the density has no lower bound.

For example, consider the truncated normal distribution on $[a,b]$, where $Y\sim \mathcal{TN}(a,b,\mu,\sigma^2)$ and $-\infty < a < b < \infty$. Then
$$
	S=-\log(2\pi\sigma^2)/2-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(Y-\mu)^2-\log\{\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)\},
$$
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function. Since $Y\in[a,b]$, $S$ is bounded for all $\sigma>0$ and hence $S$ satisfies \cref{ass:bounded}.

Under \cref{ass:bounded}, we have the following result on the maximum of the surprisals.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bounds}
	Suppose $\bm{y}_1,\dots,\bm{y}_n$ are iid from a density $f$, and let $M_n = \max\{s_1,\dots,s_n\}$ where $s_i = -\log f(\bm{y}_i)$. If \cref{ass:bounded} is satisfied, then, for $s\in\mathbb{R}_+$,
	$$
		\mathrm{Pr}(M_n-\E[S]\ge s)\le 1-\left(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}s^2/\nu^2}\right)^n.
	$$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	For any $\lambda>0$,
	\begin{align*}
		\Pr\{S\ge s\} =
		\Pr\left\{e^{\lambda (S-\E[S])}\ge e^{\lambda (s-\E[S])}\right\}
		 & \le e^{-\lambda(s-\mu)}\E e^{\lambda (S-\mu)} \\
		 & \le e^{-\lambda(s-\mu)}e^{\lambda^2\nu^2/2} .
	\end{align*}
	Now, we can maximise the RHS of the above bound in $\lambda$, which yields the optimal value $\lambda=(s-\mu)/\{M^2\}$. Plugging this in we have the bound
	$$
		\Pr\{S\ge s\}\le e^{-\frac{1}{2}(s-\mu)^2/\nu^2}.
	$$
	The stated result now follows by noting that
	\begin{align*}
		\mathrm{Pr}\left(M_n-\E[S]\ge s\right) = 1-\mathrm{Pr}\{M_n\le s\}
		 & = 1-\mathrm{Pr}\{S\le s\}^n                       \\
		 & = 1-(1-\Pr\{S\ge s\})^n                           \\
		 & \le 1-\left(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}s^2/\nu^2}\right)^n.
	\end{align*}
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:one}
	If \cref{ass:bounded} is satisfied, then, for all $s>0$,
	$$
		\mathrm{Pr}\Big(|M_n-\E[S]| \ge \sqrt{2\nu^2s} + \sqrt{2\nu^2\log(2n)}\Big) \le e^{-s}.
	$$
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
	This follows directly from the union bound:
	\begin{align*}
		\mathrm{Pr}\Big(|M_n|\ge \sqrt{2\nu^2s} + \sqrt{2\nu^2\log(n)}\Big)
		 & \le n \Pr\Big\{|S|\ge \sqrt{2}\nu^2s + \sqrt{2\nu^2\log(n)}\Big\}                                                     \\
		 & \le 2n e^{-[\sqrt{2\nu^2s}+\sqrt{2\nu^2\log(n)}]^2/(2\nu^2)}                                                          \\
		 & = 2n e^{-\frac{1}{2\nu^2}(2s\nu^2 )-\frac{2\nu^2\log(2n)}{2\nu^2}-\frac{\sqrt{2\nu^2s}\sqrt{2\nu^2\log(2n)}}{2\nu^2}} \\
		 & \le e^{-\sqrt{\log(2n)s}-s}                                                                                           \\
		 & \le e^{-s}
	\end{align*}
	The first result follows from the iid assumption on $S$ and the union bound, while the second from the assumption that $S$ is sub-Gaussian. The remaining inequalities follow from reorganising terms.
\end{proof}

\cref{cor:one} is useful as it clarifies that if the surprisal is sub-Gaussian, then the maximal surprisal will follow a reversed Weibull distribution, and thus is within the class of extreme value distributions.

\subsection{$S$ has an exponential moment}

In cases where the underlying density has full support on $\mathbb{R}$ and cannot be bounded from below, then the sub-Gaussianity condition in \cref{ass:bounded} may be violated, as the following result illustrates.

\begin{lemma}
	If $Y\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$, then $S=-\log f(Y)$ is sub-exponential. In particular,
	$$
		\E e^{\lambda(S-1/2)+(\lambda/2)\log(2\pi\sigma^2)}\le e^{\lambda^2/2+(\lambda/2)\log(2\pi\sigma^2)},\quad \forall |\lambda|<1/2.
	$$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Firstly, note that
	$$
		S=-\log f(Y)= \frac{1}{2}\log (2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{(Y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.
	$$
	and that
	$$
		\mu_S=\E[S]=-\int f(y)\log f(y)\dt y=\frac{1}{2}\{1+\log (2\pi\sigma^2)\}.
	$$ Define
	$$
		X=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(Y-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-\frac{1}{2},
	$$
	then $\E[X]=0$. Now, note that
	$$
		\E e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}(S-1)}={e^{\lambda/2\{\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\int e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}-1\right]}e^{-\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\dt y.
	$$
	Consider the change of variables $z=(y-\mu)/\sigma$ so that we have
	$$
		\E e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}(S-1)}={e^{\lambda/2\{\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}[z^2-1]}e^{-z^2/2}\dt y={e^{\lambda/2\{\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\}}}\frac{e^{-2(\lambda/2)}}{\sqrt{1-2(\lambda/2)}}
	$$for all $\lambda<1$. Further, it can be verified that
	$$
		\frac{e^{-\lambda/2}}{\sqrt{1-2(\lambda/2)}}\le e^{\lambda^2/2},\quad \forall |\lambda|<1/2.
	$$
	Hence, when we have $Y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$, then $S=-\log f(Y)$ is sub-exponential with parameter $\nu=1$ and $b=2$.
\end{proof}

Therefore, to be able to apply extreme value theory to the surprisal values, we need a version of \cref{lem:bounds} that uses sub-exponentiality.


\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bounds_sub}
	Suppose $\bm{y}_1,\dots,\bm{y}_n$ are iid from a density $f$, and let $M_n = \max\{s_1,\dots,s_n\}$ where $s_i = -\log f(\bm{y}_i)$. If \cref{ass:subbounded} is satisfied, then, for $s\in\mathbb{R}_+$,
	$$
		\mathrm{Pr}\left(M_n-\E[S]\ge s\right)\le 1-
		\begin{cases}
			(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}s^2/\nu^2})^n & \text{ if }0\le s\le \nu^2/b \\
			(1-e^{-s/2b})^n                 & \text{ if }s>\nu^2/b
		\end{cases}.
	$$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	The result follows a similar argument as for \cref{lem:bounds}, up until we maximize the tail bound. In particular, for any $\lambda>0$,
	\begin{align*}
		\Pr\left\{ S-\E[S]\ge s\right\} =
		\Pr\left\{e^{\lambda (S-\E[S])}\ge e^{\lambda s}\right\}
		 & \le e^{-\lambda s}\E e^{\lambda (S-\E[S])} \\
		 & \le e^{-\lambda s}e^{\lambda^2\nu^2/2} .
	\end{align*}
	Now, we must consider a constrained optimization to ensure the RHS exists. In particular, we know that from the proof of \cref{lem:bounds} that the unconstrained optimum is $\lambda=s/\nu^2$, which corresponds to the constrained optimum on the set $0\le t<\nu^2/b$. However, when $s\ge \nu^2/b$, the maximum is achieved at $\lambda=1/b$, and putting this together yields
	$$
		\Pr\left\{S-\E[S]\ge s\right\}\le
		\begin{cases}
			e^{-\frac{1}{2}s^2/\nu^2} & \text{ if }0\le s\le \nu^2/b \\
			e^{-s/2b}                 & \text{ if } s>\nu^2/b
		\end{cases}.
	$$
	The stated result again follows by noting that
	\begin{align*}
		\mathrm{Pr}\left\{M_n-\E[S]\ge s\right\}
		 & = 1-\mathrm{Pr}\left\{M_n\le s\right\} \\
		 & = 1-\mathrm{Pr}\left\{S\le s\right\}^n \\
		 & \le 1-
		\begin{cases}
			(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}s^2/\nu^2})^n & \text{ if } 0\le s\le \nu^2/b \\
			(1-e^{-s/2b})^n                 & \text{ if } s>\nu^2/b
		\end{cases}.
	\end{align*}
\end{proof}

Results similar to \cref{lem:bounds_sub} also hold for $\mathrm{Pr}\left\{M_n-\E[S]\le s\right\}$, and for $\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\ge s\right\}$ with an additional factor of $2$, i.e.,
$$
	\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\ge s\right\}\le 1-
	\begin{cases}
		(1-2e^{-\frac{1}{2}s^2/\nu^2})^n & \text{ if }0\le s\le \nu^2/b \\
		(1-2e^{-s/2b})^n                 & \text{ if }s>\nu^2/b
	\end{cases}.
$$

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:two}
	Under \cref{ass:subbounded}, for $s>\nu^2/b$, as $n\rightarrow\infty$
	$$
		\Pr\left(|M_n-\E[S]|\ge (2b)s+(2b)\log(2n)\right)\le 1-e^{-e^{-s}}+o(1/n).
	$$
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
	Apply \cref{ass:subbounded} to see that
	\begin{align*}
		\Pr\left(|M_n-\E[S]|\ge (2b)s+(2b)\log(2n)\right)
		 & \le 1- \left\{1-2e^{-[2bs+2b\log(2n)]/2b}\right\}^n                       \\
		 & = 1-\left(1-\frac{e^{-s}}{n}\right)^n                                     \\
		 & = 1-e^{e^{-s}}-\left[\left(1-\frac{e^{-s}}{n}\right)^n-e^{e^{-s}}\right].
	\end{align*}
	The result now follows by noting that, for all $s>0$,
	$$
		\left[\left(1-\frac{e^{-s}}{n}\right)^n-e^{e^{-s}}\right]= o(1)\le 0.
	$$
\end{proof}
\cref{cor:two} is useful as it shows that if the surprisal has a sub-exponential tail, then the resulting maximum surprisal tail probability converges to that of an extreme value distribution. In particular, we have that \cref{cor:two} implies
$$
	\sup_{s:s>\nu^2/b}\left|\Pr\left(|M_n-\mu|\ge [2bs+2b\log(2n)]\right)-\left(1-e^{e^{-s}}\right)\right|=o(1).
$$
To see that this implies that $M_n-\E[S]$, appropriately standardized, converges to an extreme value distribution, we first recall that
$$
	\Pr\{X_n>x\}-\Pr\{X>x\}=1-\Pr\{X_n\le x\}-\left[1-\Pr\{X\le x\}\right]=\Pr\{X\le x\}-\Pr\{X_n\le x\}.
$$
Hence,
$$
	\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\Pr\{X_n\le x\}-\Pr\{X\le x\}|=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\Pr\{X_n> x\}-\Pr\{X> x\}|,
$$
so that when $X_n$ and $X$ are both continuous we see that \cref{cor:two} implies that the CDF of $(M_n-\E[S]-2b\log(2n))/2b$ converges to that of the Type-1 extreme value distribution (i.e., Gumbel distribution); i.e., using \cref{cor:two} and the above we have that
$$
	\sup_{s:s>\nu^2/b}\left|\Pr\left(|M_n-\mu|\le [2bs+2b\log(2n)]\right\}-e^{e^{-s}}\right|=o(1).
$$
This is one notion of convergence in distribution.

Recall that, for $Y\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$, we have that $S=-\log f(Y)$ is sub-exponential with $\nu=1$ and $b=2$. Further,
$$
	\mu=\E[S]=-\int f(y)\log f(y)\dt y=\frac{1}{2}\{1+\log (2\pi\sigma^2)\}.
$$
Hence, \cref{lem:bounds_sub} immediately implies that, for $s>1/2$,
$$
	\Pr\left\{ M_n\ge \frac{1}{2}\{1+\log (2\pi\sigma^2)\}+4s+4\log(n)\right\} \le 1-
	(1-e^{-s}/n)^n
$$
or
$$
	\Pr\left\{ \left|M_n-\frac{1}{2}\{1+\log (2\pi\sigma^2)\}\right|\ge 4s+4\log(2n)\right\}\le 1-
	(1-e^{-s}/n)^n
$$

Consider that $\sigma^2=1$, then the mean surprisal is $\frac{1}{2}\{1+\log (2\pi\sigma^2)\}=.919$. Given $n$ observations, what is the probability that the absolute difference between the maximum surprisal and the mean is larger than four times the mean for various choices of $n$?
Using the above we have that
$$
	\Pr\left\{ |M_n-\frac{1}{2}\{1+\log (2\pi\sigma^2)\}|\ge 4*\frac{1}{2}\{1+\log (2\pi\sigma^2)\}+4\log(2n)\right\}\le 1-\left(1-\frac{e^{-n*.918}}{n}\right)^n
$$
For $n=10,20,50,100$ the estimate of the probability is between .565 and .551. However, if we let $\sigma^2=10$, then the mean surprisal is about 3.74, and, for the same value of $n$, the probabilities range from about .225 to .223. This illustrates that the surprisal probability actually decreases as $\sigma$ increases.

\subsection{$S$ has a polynomial moment}

Finally, we consider the case where the surprisal only has a polynomial moment (e.g., Student-t random variables), and derive a corresponding result to \cref{lem:bounds}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bounds_poly}
	Suppose $\bm{y}_1,\dots,\bm{y}_n$ are iid from a density $f$, and let $M_n = \max\{s_1,\dots,s_n\}$ where $s_i = -\log f(\bm{y}_i)$. If \cref{ass:polybounded} is satisfied, then, for $s>0$ and such that $s>C>0$,
	$$
		\mathrm{Pr}\left(|M_n-\E[S]|\ge s\right) \le 1- (1-C^p/s^p)^n.
	$$
	Hence,
	$$
		\sup_{s:s>C} \left|\mathrm{Pr}\big(|M_n-\E[S]|\le (s\cdot n^{1/p})\big)-e^{-s^{-p}}\right|=o(1).
	$$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	By Markov's inequality,
	$$
		\Pr\left\{ |S-\E[S]|\ge s\right\}\le \frac{\E|S|^p}{s^p}\le \frac{C^p}{s^p}.
	$$
	To derive the first stated result, again note that
	\begin{align*}
		\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\ge s\right\}
		 & = 1 -\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\le s\right\}               \\
		 & = 1-\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|S-\E[S]|\le s\right\}^n                \\
		 & = 1-\left(1-\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|S-\E[S]|\ge s\right\}\right)^n \\
		 & \le 1-(1-C^p/s^p)^n.
	\end{align*}

	To derive the second stated result, apply the first result with $t=sn^{1/p}C>1$, which clearly holds for some $n$ large enough, to see that
	$$
		\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\ge t\right\}
		\le 1- \left(1-\frac{C^p}{(Csn^{1/p})^p}\right)^n \\
		= 1-\left(1-n^{-1}s^{-p}\right)^n.
	$$
	Now, recall that, for $x\ge0$,
	$$
		\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(1-\frac{x}{n}\right)^n=e^{-x}.
	$$
	Since $x\mapsto e^{-x}$ is bounded for all $x\ge0$, it follows that
	$$
		\sup_{s:s>C}\left|\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\ge t\right\}-e^{-s^{-p}}\right|=o(1).
	$$
\end{proof}

\subsection{Three types theorem}

Placing the three lemmas together gives the following ``three types'' Theorem.
\textbf{\color{blue}Note the change here to $\le$ from $\ge$. Check its correct in the paper version.}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:three_types}
	Let $\bm{y}_1,\dots,\bm{y}_n$ be an iid sequence from $F$, and let $S =-\log f(\bm{Y})$, where $Y\sim \bm{F}$. Further, let $M_n=\max\{s_1,\dots,s_n\}$, where $s_i=-\log f(y_i)$.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item If the surprisal satisfies \cref{ass:bounded}, then
		      $$
			      \sup_{s:s>0}\left|\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\le \sqrt{2\nu^2 s}+\sqrt{2\nu^2\log(2n)}\right\}-e^{-s}\right|=o(1).
		      $$
		\item If the surprisal satisfies \cref{ass:subbounded}, then
		      $$
			      \sup_{s:s>1/b}\left|\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\le (2b)s+(2b)\log(2n)\right\}-e^{-e^{-s}}\right|=o(1).
		      $$
		\item If the surprisal satisfies \cref{ass:polybounded}, then
		      $$
			      \sup_{s:s>C}\left|\mathrm{Pr}\left\{|M_n-\E[S]|\le (Csn^{1/p})\right\}-e^{-s^{-p}}\right|=o(1).
		      $$
	\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\cref{thm:three_types} gives a corresponding `three types' theorem for the behavior of surprisals and has a simple interpretation. If the surprisal has Gaussian like tails, then the maximum surprisal is a reversed Weibull; if it only has an exponential moment, then the maximum surprisal will be Gumbel; if the maximum surprisal only has a polynomial moment, then it is Frechet.


\subsubsection{Type (3) Approach: asymptotic comparison}
Theorem \ref{thm:three_types} explains that the distribution of $M_n$ is the generalized extreme value distribution: for $z=(s-\mu)/\sigma$,
$$
	\Pr\{(M_n-\mu)/\sigma\le z\}=\mathrm{Gev}\{(s-\mu)/\sigma;0,1,\xi\}=\begin{cases}
	\exp \left\{-\left(1+\xi\cdot z\right)^{-1 / \xi}\right\} &\xi>0\\\exp(-z)&\xi=0,    
	\end{cases}
$$
wheree $\mu \in \Re, \sigma>0$ and $\xi \in \mathbb{R}$. This distribution takes specific forms depending on the particular value of the shape parameter $\xi$ and changes depending on this value. 
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The (reverse) Weibull distribution is obtained when $\xi<0$.
	\item The Gumble distirbution is obtained when $\xi=0$.
	\item The Frechet distribution is obtained when $\xi>0$.
\end{enumerate}

For $n$ large and $z$ large, the tail probability can then be generically expressed as 
$$
\Pr\{(M_n-\mu)/\sigma>z\}= 1-e^{-t(z)}+o(1),\quad t(z)=\begin{cases}
(1+\xi \cdot z)^{-\frac{1}{\xi}}&\xi\ne0\\\exp(-z)&\xi=0 . 
\end{cases}
$$
An immediately useful implication of Theorem \ref{thm:three_types} is that it gives us a direct way of linking the disagreement between the tail behavior of the surprisal that is fit under the assumed model, $\hat{f}(y)$, and the surprisal that would be fit under the ``true model'', denoted by $f^\star(y)$. 

To see this implication, let $S^\star=-\log f^\star(Y)$, and $\hat{S}=-\log \hat{f}(Y)$ denote the surprisals associated to the random variable $Y$ under the assumed and true models, respectively. Further, let $\hat{M}_n$ and $M_n^\star$ be the maximal counterparts, and define $\hat\mu=\E(\hat{S})$, $\mu^\star=\E (S^\star)$, and $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{S})}$ and $\sigma^\star=\sqrt{\text{Var}(S^\star)}$. Then, Theorem \ref{thm:three_types} directly implies that, for $n$ and $z$ large, 
\begin{flalign}
\left|\Pr\{(\hat{M}_n-\hat\mu)/\hat\sigma>z\}-\Pr\{(M^\star_n-\mu^\star)/\sigma^\star>z\}\right|&=|e^{-\hat{t}(z)}-e^{-t^\star(z)}|+o(1)\nonumber\\&=e^{-t^\star(z)}|1-e^{-\left\{\hat{t}(z)-t^\star(z)\right\}}|    +o(1),\label{eq:new1} 
\end{flalign}where 
$\hat{t}(z)$ (resp., $t^\star(z)$) denotes $t(z)$ with $\xi=\hat\xi$ (resp., $\xi=\xi^\star$), and where $\hat\xi$ and $\xi^\star$ exist (for $n$ large enough) by Theorem \ref{thm:three_types}. 

Equation \eqref{eq:new1} shows that, even though the fitted model $\hat{f}$ may be misspecified, we can still attain a bound on the accuracy between the fitted surprisal distribution and the true surprisal distribution:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:new_bound}
\left|\Pr\{(\hat{M}_n-\hat\mu)/\hat\sigma>z\}-\Pr\{(M^\star_n-\mu^\star)/\sigma^\star>z\}\right|\le e^{-t^\star(z)}+o(1)
\end{equation}
 so long as
$$
1-e^{-\left\{\hat{t}(z)-t^\star(z)\right\}}\le1\iff \hat{t}(z)\ge t^\star(z)\iff \hat\xi\ge\xi^\star.
$$
Equation \eqref{eq:new_bound} implies that so long as the tails of surprisal under the assumed model, $\hat{S}=-\log \hat{f}(Y)$, are close to that of the surprisal under the true model, $S^\star=-\log f^\star(Y)$, the two tail probabilities will be close. Hence, we only have to ensure that the assumed model has tails that ``resemble the observed data'' to ensure we can accurately detect surprisals using our proposed framework. 


We can write the result formally as a corollary to Theorem \ref{thm:three_types}. 
\begin{corollary}\label{corr:new_corr}
Under Assumptions 1-3, if $\hat\xi\ge \xi^\star$,  then for $z$ large and $n$ large,   
$$
\left|\Pr\{(\hat{M}_n-\hat\mu)/\hat\sigma>z\}-\Pr\{(M^\star_n-\mu^\star)/\sigma^\star>z\}\right|\le e^{-t^\star(z)}+o(1).
$$
\end{corollary}



For example, if the true model is $N(-1,10)$ and we use a $N(0,1)$ assumed model, then the (normalized) estimated surprisal distribution has the same tail thickness as the true surprisal distribution and would therefore deliver a reasonably accurate approximation. Alternatively, if the true data is $\chi^2(d)$ and we maintain the same $N(0,1)$ assumed model, then we will not accurately detect surprisals since the tails of the true distributions are substantially different.

\subsection{Empirical surprisal probabilities}

Under approach (2) above, we estimate $p_i=\Pr[S> S_i]$ as the proportion of observed surprisals larger than $s_i$. Using this approach, the anomaly boundary for a threshold of $\alpha$ corresponds to the $100(1-\alpha)$\% highest density region computed using the quantile density approach of @HDR96.

For this approach to be accurate the surprisal ranks under the actual distribution generating $Y$, denoted by $F(\cdot)$ with generalized density function $f(\cdot)$, and the distribution used to model $Y$, which is denoted by $\widehat{F}$ with generalized density function $\widehat{f}(\cdot)$, must behave similarly. In particular, \textit{it is both necessary and sufficient} that the surprisal ranks in the upper tail of the surprisal distribution, since we are only interested in large surprisal values,  can be related through a monotone transformation.

Likewise, let $\widehat{S}=-\log \widehat{f}(\bm{Y})$ denote the surprisal under the fitted distribution, and for $\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_n(s):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1[\widehat{S}_i\le s]$ the empirical CDF of the fitted sample $\widehat{S}_1,\dots,\widehat{S}_n$ let $\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_n(s)=1-\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_n(s)$ denote the empirical survival function. \begin{assumption}\label{ass:empirical_pis}
There exists an $s_\star$ such that, for all $s\ge s_\star$ and a function $h(\cdot)$, strictly increasing on $s\ge s_\star$, such that $S=h(\widehat{S})$ almost surely on the set $\{S\ge s_\star\}\cap\{\widehat{S}\ge h^{-1}(s_\star)\}$. 
\end{assumption}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:confident}
Assume that $S_1,\dots, S_n$ are iid realizations from $F$. For $\alpha \in(0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\alpha$, for any $s \ge s_\star$, $\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_n(h^{-1}(s))-\epsilon \leq \Pr(S>s) \leq \widehat{\mathbb{S}}_n(h^{-1}(s))+\epsilon$ if an only if Assumption \ref{ass:empirical_pis} is satisfied.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}

\begin{flalign}

\end{align*}
Hence, with probability at least $1-\alpha$, $\sup_{s \ge s_\star}|\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_n(h^{-1}(s))-\Pr(S>s)|\le\epsilon$. Fixing an $s\ge s_\star$ and re-arranging terms yields the stated inequality

Lemma \ref{lem:confident} implies that if the fitted distribution has tails that are similarly located to the actual data distribution, then the fitted empirical surprisal probabilities are very accurate measures of the true surprisal probability, $G(s)$. Critically, this does not require that the tails of the fitted distribution are the same as the actual data distribution, and only requires that they be related through a monotone transformation. That is, for this approach to be accurate, we must be confident that the fitted distribution has regions of low density in the same location as the true distribution.

\section{\cref{thm:three_types} in Action}

In this section, we provide a few numerical experiments that illustrate the validity of \cref{thm:three_types}. The essence of \cref{thm:three_types} is that, as $n\rightarrow\infty$, the distribution of $M_n$ behaves like a generalized extreme value distribution,
$$
	\mathrm{Gev}(x;\mu,\sigma,\xi)=\exp \left\{-\left(1+\xi \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-1 / \xi}\right\},
$$
defined when $1+\xi(x-\mu) / \sigma>0 ; \mu \in \Re, \sigma>0$ and $\xi \in \mathbb{R}$. This distribution takes specific forms depending on the particular value of the shape parameter $\xi$ and changes depending on this value.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The (reverse) Weibull distribution is obtained when $\xi<0$.
	\item The Gumble distirbution is obtained when $\xi=0$.
	\item The Frechet distribution is obtained when $\xi>0$.
\end{enumerate}
Thus, in order to validate the results of \cref{thm:three_types}, all we need to show is that:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item when $S$ is sub-Gaussian, the distribution of the maximum surprisal $M_n$ has shape parameter $\xi<0$;
	\item when $S$ is sub-exponential, the distribution of the maximum surprisal $M_n$ has shape parameter $\xi=0$;
	\item when $S$ only has a polynomial moment, the distribution of the maximum surprisal $M_n$ has shape parameter $\xi>0$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Truncated Normal distribution}

Let us return to the truncated normal distribution example. Consider that we observe data generated according to
$$
	Y_i\stackrel{iid}{\sim} \mathcal{TN}(a=1,b=5,\mu=2,\sigma=1)\;(i=1,\dots,n).
$$
In this example, since $Y\in[1,5]$, it is simple to show that surprisal $S_i$ is bounded. Hence, we would expect that $M_n=\max\{S_1,\dots,S_n\}$ should eventually resemble a GEV distribution where the shape parameter $\xi<0$. To verify this, we generate $R=1000$ independent samples of size $n=1000$ from the truncated normal distribution and for each $r=1,\dots,1000$ calculate $M_n^{(r)}=\max\{S_1^{(r)},\dots,S_n^{(r)}\}$. We plot the histogram of $\{M_n^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{1000}$ and compare this with the fitted GEV distribution in \cref{fig:one}. The distribution seems very close to the (reversed) Weibull distribution, and indeed the estimated parameters from this experiment yield an estimated shape parameter $\hat\xi_R=-0.2002$, with a standard error of 0.0271, suggesting high confidence that the resulting estimated parameter is negative and that the distribution of $M_n$ is indeed Weibull.

\subsection{Normal distribution}

Now, let us return to the normal example, which, from our theoretical results, we know should give rise to surprisals that exhibit Gumbel like behavior. To see that this is indeed the case, consider that we observe data generated according to
$$
	Y_i\stackrel{iid}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu=1,\sigma=1)\;(i=1,\dots,n).
$$
In this example, we know that the surprisal $S_i$ will have a sub-exponential tail and that $M_n=\max\{S_1,\dots,S_n\}$ should eventually resemble a GEV distribution where the shape parameter $\xi=0$. To verify this, we generate $R=1000$ independent samples of size $n=1000$ from the normal distribution with mean and variance both equal to unity, and for each $r=1,\dots,1000$ we calculate $M_n^{(r)}=\max\{S_1^{(r)},\dots,S_n^{(r)}\}$. We plot the histogram of $\{M_n^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{1000}$ and compare this with the fitted GEV distribution in \cref{fig:two}. The estimated parameters from this experiment yield an estimated shape parameter $\hat\xi_R=0.0012$, with a standard error of 0.0209, suggesting high confidence that the actual shape parameter is either zero or very close to it, and that the distribution of $M_n$ is indeed Gumbel.

\subsection{Student-t distribution}
For the last example, we consider the situation where the assumed model is Gaussian with mean and variance equal to unity, but where the observed data is actually generated from a student-t distribution with five degrees of freedom. In this case, the tails of the student-t ensure that the surprisal does not have an exponential moment, and, as discussed in \cref{thm:three_types}, the distribution of the maximum surprisal, $M_n$, will be Frechet.

To show that this is the case we generate $R=1000$ independent samples of size $n=1000$ from a student-t distribution with five degrees of freedom, but where the mean and variance both equal to unity, and for each $r=1,\dots,1000$ we calculate $M_n^{(r)}=\max\{S_1^{(r)},\dots,S_n^{(r)}\}$. We plot the histogram of $\{M_n^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{1000}$ and compare this with the fitted GEV distribution in \cref{fig:three}. The estimated parameters from this experiment yield an estimated shape parameter $\hat\xi_R=0.4557$, with a standard error of 0.0294, suggesting high confidence that the actual shape parameter is positive, and that the distribution of $M_n$ is indeed Frechet.

The most interesting thing from these examples from a methodological perspective is that if we get the assumed distribution of the data wrong, then this will have meaningful consequence for the behavior of the surprisal we are calculating. In particular, as the student-t example shows, when we model the data using a distribution which is assumed to have thinner tails than those of the actual data, the surprisal can have a very long/thick tail. This immediately suggests that if our goal is to check for anomalies, we should be using distributions that are somewhat robust to outliers, e.g., student-t, etc. I've obtained similar results if you model the data with a Gaussian, but the actual data generation mechanism is exponential. In this case, the max surprisal is Frechet. However, if you had modeled the data with something like a heavy-tailed student-t, then the max surprisal is Gumble. This suggests that the model you specify is absolutely critical to understanding behavior of the max surprisal itself.


\section{Empirical CDF Approach}
Recall that the surprisal is $S=-\log f(Y)$, for some assumed distribution $F$ with density/mass function $f(\cdot)$, and recall that $G(s)=\Pr(S\le s)$ denotes the CDF of $S$ at $s\in\mathbb{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$, where $\mathcal{S}$ denotes the support of the surprisals. Assume that the sequence of observed surprisals $s_1,\dots,s_n$ are iid realization from $S$, and let $\mathbb{P}_n(s):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1[s_i\le s]$ denote the empirical CDF (eCDF) of the realized sample $s_1,\dots,s_n$. The following result shows that the eCDF of the surprisals can be used to consistently detect surprisals even though $G$ is unknown and $Y$ may not be distributed according to $F$. 
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ecdf}
Assume that $s_1,\dots,s_n$ are iid realization from $S\sim G$. Let $\epsilon=\sqrt{\log (2/\alpha)/(2n)}$. For $\alpha\in(0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\alpha$, for any $s\in\mathbb{S}$,
$
\mathbb{P}_n(s)-\epsilon\le G(s)\le \mathbb{P}_n(s)+\epsilon.
$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
From the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality, for every $\epsilon>0$, we have 
$$
\Pr\left\{\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|\mathbb{P}_n(s)-G(s)|>\epsilon\right\}\le 2e^{-2n\epsilon^2}.
$$Taking $\epsilon$ as in the statement of the result we see that
\begin{flalign*}
\Pr\left\{\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|\mathbb{P}_n(s)-G(s)|>\epsilon\right\}&\le 2e^{-2n\epsilon^2}\\&=2e^{-2n \left(\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{2n}\right)}\\&=2e^{-\log(2/\alpha)}\\&=\alpha.    
\end{flalign*}
Hence, with probability at least $1-\alpha$, $\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|\mathbb{P}_n(s)-G(s)|\le\epsilon$. Fixing an $s\in\mathcal{S}$ and re-arranging terms yields the stated inequality
$$
\mathbb{P}_n(s)-\epsilon\le G(s)\le \mathbb{P}_n(s)+\epsilon,
$$where $\epsilon$ is as stated in the result.
\end{proof}

Since $p_i=1-G(s_i)=\Pr[S>s_i]$, Lemma \ref{lem:ecdf} gives us an immediate bound on the accuracy of $p_i$. Namely, if we choose $\epsilon$ as in Lemma \ref{lem:ecdf}, then with probability at least $1-\alpha$
$$
1-\mathbb{P}_n(s_i)-\epsilon\le p_i\le 1-\mathbb{P}_n(s_i)+\epsilon
$$


This result by itself is not necessarily useful. After all, we want to conduct inference on the true distribution of the true surprisal: 
$$
G^\star(s)=\Pr(S^\star\le s)=\Pr(-\log f^\star(Y)\le s),\quad Y\sim f^\star.
$$In general, all $\mathbb{P}_n(s)$ estimates is the distribution of the ``fitted surprisal'' 
$$
G(s)=\Pr(-\log f(Y)\le s)\ne G^\star(s)
$$
In general, there is no reason to expect that $G(s)$ should be close to $G^\star(s)$ for all $s$. 

That being said, if we wish to have a conservative bound for the surprisal, all we require is that 
$$
\Pr(S\ge s)\ge \Pr(S^\star\ge s)\iff 1-G(s)\ge 1-G^\star(s)\iff G(s)\le G^\star(s)
$$
If $S$ satisfies $G(s)\le G^\star(s)$ for all $s\in\mathcal{S}$, and $G(s)< G^\star(s)$ for at least some $s\in\mathcal{S}$, then we say that $S$ \textbf{first-order stochastically dominates} $S^\star$. 

Hence, so long as $S$ FOSD $S^\star$, we have that 
$$
\Pr(S^\star\ge s_i)=1-G^\star(s_i)\le p_i=1-G(s_i)\le 1-\mathbb{P}_n(s_i)+\epsilon,
$$by our previous result with probability at least $1-\alpha$.
